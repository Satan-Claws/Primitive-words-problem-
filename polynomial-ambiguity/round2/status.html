Primitive words and polynomially ambiguous grammars

Can a context-free grammar generate exactly the primitive words while giving every word only polynomially many parse trees?

1. The problemWhat exactly is being asked

Primitive words

A word is primitive if it is not a repetition of a shorter word.

  • aab, abaab: primitive
  • abab = (ab)², aaa = a³: not primitive

Q = the set of primitive words over {a, b}. Almost every word is primitive; the non-primitive ones are rare but structured.

Ambiguity

A grammar may derive one word in many ways. G is polynomially ambiguous if every word w has ≤ C·|w|ᵏ parse trees, for constants C and k of the grammar.

Question: is there such a G with L(G) = Q?

Wich's dichotomy: every grammar is either polynomially or exponentially ambiguous, so "no" means "every grammar for Q is exponentially ambiguous".

Context. Whether Q is context-free at all is a famous open problem (Dömösi–Horváth–Ito, 1991). Known: Q is not unambiguous (Petersen 1996), and not finitely ambiguous (proved in this repo, Lean-checked). Polynomial ambiguity is the next rung on that ladder: it is strictly harder to rule out than finite ambiguity, and strictly easier than settling context-freeness.

3. What the idea isThe mechanism behind both directions

Primitivity is a "for every prime" statement

A word w is not primitive exactly when w = vᵖ for a prime p dividing gcd(#a, #b). So a proof that w is primitive must, for every such prime p, show that w is not a p-th power.

For one fixed prime this is easy for a grammar: point to one mismatch between positions i and i + n/p. The grammar enforces the distance with an outside:inside length ratio of (p−1):1. Ambiguity is linear.

ajax's plan

Force a blow-up on slices

Pick special families of words, slices (ab*)ⁿ with n = (product of first N primes)², where certifying every prime seems to need superpolynomially many overlapping parse trees.

round 2 finding

Slices don't blow up

Those slices can be covered with overlap 1 (transport tiling), using pieces a fixed grammar could produce. The slice route cannot prove "no". audited

rounds 3–5 finding

The crux is uniformity

The real constraint is that one grammar must work for every length. A grammar can't expose certificates for two primes, or for a varying prime. The words that are actually hard are typical random words.

Current working picture. Every certificate a context-free grammar can obviously check (letter-count relations, factor counts, folds) covers only a vanishing fraction (~n−1/2) of words. The single exception found is the interval-fold certificate: it compares the a-counts of the prefix and suffix of the same length k. If w = uᵖ, at k = ⌊p/2⌋·n/p the prefix and suffix are equal, and ⌊p/2⌋/p always lies in [1/3, 1/2]. So one window checks every prime at once and covers about 61% of words. But it needs a counter running along a fold, which may be beyond context-free grammars. Whether it can be made context-free is the question everything now turns on.

7. Open questionsRanked by how directly they move the problem

1FOLD-COUNTER: can one grammar make the length coupling work?sharpened in round 6

After round 6 the question is very specific. A rotation of almost every primitive word lies in G⁻ = {prefix a-count < suffix a-count for every k ≤ n/2}, which is inside Q. Exact cyclic closure turns any grammar for a part of G⁻ into one for all its rotations. G⁻ splits as a Dyck-type prefix times an a-heavy suffix, glued by one length condition |x| ≥ |y|. Each half is easy for a grammar; the coupling is the whole problem. Dropping it lets squares like baa·baa through. Finite-memory checks provably fail (Prop K), and the coupled language L_A is not context-free (Prop N). Whether some dense polynomially ambiguous sublanguage exists is open. The negative side needs "Lemma Q", a density bound inside shifted meanders.

If yes: sound polynomial grammars cover most typical words. The "generic words are hard" heuristic dies, and the remaining difficulty is the structured exceptions. That moves the problem toward "yes".
If no, for a robust reason ("fold + counter is never CF on dense sets"): the strongest negative lead so far. It explains why every known certificate stays at density → 0.
2P_cop: the minimal sandwich

Is there a polynomially ambiguous language that contains every word whose letter counts are coprime and contains only primitive words? By Prop H (audited) this is the weakest statement in the chain:

grammar for Q ⇒ Q∩{gcd = prime power} ⇒ Q∩{gcd = p} ⇒ P_cop

If P_cop holds: nothing follows directly. It is a necessary condition only.
If P_cop fails: the problem is solved as no. The proof must use the ambiguity bound, since pumping alone would prove Q isn't CF.
3L₆: the smallest two-prime test

Words of length 6m that are neither squares nor cubes. Each condition alone has a linear-ambiguity grammar; together, no grammar is known. Its generating function is rational, so counting arguments can't decide it.

If L₆ has a polynomial grammar: it gives a template for combining primes, the core affirmative mechanism.
If not: a clean obstruction, though it doesn't transfer to Q automatically (L₆ ≠ Q ∩ lengths 6ℕ).
4NONUNIF: can one grammar realise the slice covers for every length?

Every slice has a low-overlap cover (audited), but the covers we know are built separately for each n. Whether a single finite grammar can produce them for all n is equivalent to the slice-based negative statement. Two attempts (Theorem R, forbidden exchange) collapsed to known routes.

5SPREAD-JOINT: the last per-slice gap

A family of spread facets whose witness chords don't cross, covering mixed-type defects. It would finish blocking S_N completely, but can't resolve the problem alone.

8. How this leads to a solutionTwo routes, one decision point

Route to "yes"

  1. Make a dense part of the fold certificate context-free (FOLD-COUNTER).
  2. Combine primes without exposing witnesses (L₆ is the test case).
  3. Union with the uniform single-prime grammars and the known covers; prove L(G) = Q for all lengths and bound the trees.

A "yes" would show Q is context-free, refuting a 35-year-old conjecture, so it would need extreme scrutiny.

Route to "no"

  1. Prove dense fold-type certificates are not context-free, for a reason that generalises.
  2. Use polynomial ambiguity (Wich's structure theory) to show any grammar for Q must contain such a certificate on typical words.
  3. Conclude via P_cop: no polynomially ambiguous sandwich exists.

Step 2 is the hardest. Any argument that ignores ambiguity would prove Q isn't context-free, which is harder and not the target.
