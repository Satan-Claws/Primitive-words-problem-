Primitive words and polynomially ambiguous grammars

Can one context-free grammar generate exactly the words that are not repetitions, while giving every word only polynomially many parse trees?

In one minuteWhat is being done right now, and why

The question. A word like abab is a repetition (ab twice), while aab is primitive. Is there a grammar for exactly the primitive words that is only polynomially ambiguous? Most experts expect no.

The approach. Instead of hunting for special bad words (the original plan, which turned out not to work), we look at ordinary random words. We found one simple test, a fold of the word, that proves almost every word is primitive. So the problem becomes: can a grammar carry out the fold test? If not even an unambiguous grammar can, that's strong evidence for "no", and a template for the full proof.

Where we are. We're proving T8_unamb: no unambiguous grammar can carry out the fold test on a sizeable fraction of words. The proof splits into cases. All are done except one (one-sided heavy-tailed grammars), and most of the finished pieces have passed an independent audit.

Every case and every foundation has passed a gated audit. No unambiguous grammar can carry out the fold test on a positive fraction of words. 215,820-grammar counterexample search: none. The round-16 "quadrant" argument has now passed its audit.

Lift from unambiguous to polynomially ambiguous. Round 17 showed the squeeze alone tops out below n/log n, so a new ingredient is needed. Then show every grammar for Q must contain a fold-type part.

1. The problemDefinitions, and where it sits

Primitive words

A word is primitive if it isn't a shorter word repeated: aab, abaab are primitive; abab = (ab)², aaa = a³ are not. Q is the set of primitive words over {a, b}. Almost every word is primitive; the exceptions are rare but highly structured.

Ambiguity

A grammar can derive the same word in several ways. It is polynomially ambiguous if every word w has at most C·|w|ᵏ parse trees. By Wich's dichotomy, "no" means every grammar for Q is exponentially ambiguous.

The ladder of difficulty

2. What we're doing, step by stepThe current strategy, from the problem down to the case we're working on

1

Turn "primitive" into something a grammar could check done

A word is a repetition exactly when w = vᵖ for a prime p dividing both letter counts. So a grammar has to certify, for every such prime at once, that w isn't a p-th power. For a single prime this is easy (point at one mismatch between positions i and i + n/p; linear ambiguity). The whole difficulty is all primes, one grammar, every length.

We also showed the original plan (structured "slice" families that would force huge ambiguity) cannot work: those slices can be covered with overlap 1 audited. And the hard words turned out to be typical random words: every simple certificate a grammar can check covers a vanishing fraction of them (~n−1/2).

2

Find one test that certifies almost every word: the fold done · audited

Fold the word in half. For each k, compare a's in the first k letters with a's in the last k letters, and call the difference dk.

  • If w = uᵖ, then at k = ⌊p/2⌋·n/p the first k letters and the last k letters are the same word, so dk = 0. That k always lies between n/3 and n/2, for every prime p.
  • So "dk ≠ 0 for every k in [n/3, n/2]" proves primitivity for all primes in one go (Lemma F) audited. About 61% of words pass.
  • After rotating the word to a canonical starting point, almost every primitive word satisfies the cleaner condition G⁻: dk < 0 for every k ≤ n/2 (Lemma A) audited. Rotations are free for grammars (exact cyclic closure).
3

Pin down what's hard about the fold for grammars done

A grammar can easily generate a word as nested pairs (first letter with last letter, and so on inwards). What it cannot obviously do is count along the fold. We showed G⁻ is exactly two simple halves glued by one length condition:

Every attempt to avoid the coupling recreates the original problem one level down (the self-similarity results U1–U3). Finite-memory versions provably fail (Prop K). So the real question is: can any grammar implement this length coupling on a dense set of words?

4

Prove no unambiguous grammar can (T8_unamb) done · fully audited

Statement: no unambiguous context-free language inside G⁻ contains a rotation of a positive fraction of all words of length n, for all large n.

Method: a "measure squeeze".

  1. A grammar covering that many words must contain a critical, heavy-tailed block structure: long blocks generated on each side of the fold centre (Thm M, Lemma Bot).
  2. Those blocks can be swapped freely, so the grammar can make one side "flat" for as long as it likes. Staying inside G⁻ then traps every word's walk in a narrow region: a wedge, or a quadrant.
  3. Random walks rarely stay in narrow regions (probability ~n−2/3 or ~1/n). But an unambiguous grammar with that block structure has to produce many distinct words there (≥ Nβ). Too few words fit, which is a contradiction.

What T8_unamb does and doesn't give. It shows the single best-known way to certify typical words is out of reach for unambiguous grammars. That is strong evidence for "no". But it doesn't solve the problem: round 17 showed that getting from "fold-type grammars" to "every grammar" is as hard as the problem itself (step 5b).

The proof splits by where the long blocks sit (one side or both) and how heavy their length distribution is. See the proof map for each case's status.

5

Lift to polynomial ambiguity, then to every grammar in progress

  • 5a. From unambiguous to polynomially ambiguous. Partly done: Prop P extends T8 whenever the critical component has an unambiguous nonterminal; the gap is "G-trunk". Also found: the squeeze alone cannot finish it. Its gaps are polynomial, so it can never rule out ambiguity of about n/log n or more (ceiling lemma), and a single erased marker already costs a factor n. A full lift needs a new ingredient: a set-level density bound inside the quadrant (G-word), or using G⁻ to force exponential ambiguity directly (G-struct).
  • 5b. From fold-type grammars to every grammar for Q. Round 17 finding: this "bridge" is equivalent to the whole problem.
    • Regular filtering can't do it (Prop NR).
    • The weakest property that would let T8 apply (a critical sub-grammar staying inside G⁻) is exactly what T8 forbids (Lemma Core).
    • A bridge must exploit completeness (the grammar generates every primitive word) together with ambiguity. No mechanism for that is known yet.
    Related targets: P_cop and EQ-2q (see open questions).

If at any point a grammar does implement the fold densely, the picture flips toward "yes": the remaining words are rare structured exceptions that known gadgets largely handle.

1Lifting to polynomial ambiguity (T8_poly): now the main target

Every negative tool works best for unambiguous grammars. Round 17: the measure squeeze has a hard ceiling. It can never exclude ambiguity ≳ n/log n, because all its gaps are polynomial. The quadrant itself is exactly as large as rotation-density needs (~1/n). What could work: G-word, showing a grammar can't fill a positive share of the quadrant words at the set level, or G-struct, using G⁻ to force a pair of distinct pumping trees with the same interface (Wich's criterion for exponential ambiguity).

Round 17 progress (Prop P, written proof): in a polynomially ambiguous grammar, every self-branching component must be unambiguous, and all ambiguity sits on a bounded number of linear "trunk" spines (Lemmas IF, SU, Trunk Lemma). So T8 does extend to polynomial ambiguity whenever the critical component contains an unambiguous nonterminal. Remaining gap (G-trunk): the critical component lies on the trunk and is fully ambiguous.

Round 17, G-word (written proof, works at ANY ambiguity): inserting a long flat word on the outside moves the fold centre into it. Then the quadrant condition constrains the whole word, so such words have density ~n−3/2, and even their rotations only reach ~1/√n: not rotation-dense. Gap (G-word′): this is proved for words with bounded surrounding context. One must show a dense grammar's words pass through such a structure with small context. If so, the lift is done without needing injectivity at all.

Round 17, G-word′ progress: the bound tolerates far more context than first thought, up to h = o(n/log n) (Lemma FR-pos, tolerance T-tol). Thm G-geo: T8 holds at any polynomial ambiguity whenever every critical component has flat loops on both sides and geometrically distributed context. Remaining gap (G-end): stacked critical components, where the context is a constant fraction of n. This overlaps with G-trunk.

G-trunk reduced (round 17): the only remaining multiplicity is the exit sliding inside the critical component. It needs only the average multiplicity to be small, condition (AM): E[m] = o(Nβ₀). The worst case can be larger (Example S has ~N trees on one word), and the ceiling lemma doesn't apply to averages. Lemma NR (two different parses never re-synchronise) is the starting point.

3EQ-2q: match Q exactly on rows with 2q a's

Is there a polynomially ambiguous grammar that agrees exactly with Q on every row with 2q a's (q prime)? If not, the problem is solved as "no". The bad words there are only squares and q-th powers of two-a words; Lemma S characterises the good ones exactly. The missing piece is making the ambiguity bound enter.

4P_cop: the minimal sandwich

Is there a polynomially ambiguous language containing every coprime-count word and only primitive words? It is the weakest statement in the chain P(Q) ⇒ … ⇒ P_cop (Prop H, audited). Theorems N and N2 (audited) show "row" arguments can't refute it, so any proof must also handle typical words.

5Older forms (lower priority)
  • L₆: length-6m words that are neither squares nor cubes. Its generating function is rational, so counting can't decide it.
  • Self-similar depth uniformity, and the per-slice gaps SPREAD-JOINT / NONUNIF. None can resolve the problem alone.
